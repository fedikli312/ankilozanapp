## 1-3. Naming stress test — Ilium

**Note on how this section was produced:** the dedicated adversarial research subagent for this section failed mid-run on a session-wide rate limit (not a content problem — it had barely started). Rather than wait or re-spawn another subagent into the same limit, this session ran the adversarial searches directly and reasoned through the result itself. That turned out to matter: it surfaced findings materially more serious than Phase DESIGN-A's original, lighter-touch spot-check found, and this section shows that reasoning honestly, including a point where the recommendation was seriously reconsidered before landing on a final, qualified answer — not a reflexive re-confirmation.

### 1. Aggressive collision research — real findings, not a repeat of the round-1 spot-check

**"Ilium Software" — a real, active, established company.** Founded 1997, based in Plymouth, Michigan — a real, currently operating independent software vendor selling a family of consumer/business apps (a password manager, list-management tools) across iOS, Android, macOS, and Windows, with an active company website, LinkedIn page, Facebook page, Amazon storefront, and Dun & Bradstreet company profile. Round 1's spot-check called this "a legacy, non-health desktop-software brand... negligible consumer-confusion risk" — that characterization undersold it: the company is not legacy/dead, it is a live, still-selling software brand using the exact string "Ilium" as its name. This is a real collision, though in an unrelated category (productivity/password software, not health) — the practical risk is diluted App Store/web search results for the bare word "Ilium," not brand confusion for someone who already knows this is a health app.

**"Ilium" vs. "Ileum" — a real, well-documented medical-terminology confusion.** Direct research confirms this is not a manufactured concern: medical-education sources describe ilium/ileum as having "caus[ed] great confusion to medical students and junior doctors alike for decades," differing by one letter, with pronunciation that can be identical or nearly so, and dedicated mnemonics exist specifically because the mix-up is common ("E = Enteric/intestine, I = Iliac/pelvis"). The *ileum* is a completely different organ (part of the small intestine). This is a genuine, evidence-backed risk, specifically relevant to this product's own audience: patients who discuss their condition with clinicians, and any clinically-literate person (a rheumatologist, a nurse, a medical-coding-literate reviewer) encountering the brand for the first time.

**"Ilium" as a real literary/mythological place name — a real, and larger-than-expected, cultural association.** "Ilium" is the actual Latin/Roman name for **Troy** — not a loose adjacency, the direct historical name. Kurt Vonnegut used "Ilium, New York" as a recurring fictional setting across multiple major, widely-read novels (*Player Piano*, *Cat's Cradle*, and referenced in *Slaughterhouse-Five*), explicitly invoking the Troy association for satirical effect. For an English-literate audience — and specifically for anyone with a literature background, which is not a negligible slice of a premium consumer app's potential audience or press coverage — "Ilium" may register first as "the word for Troy" or "that Vonnegut town," not as an anatomical reference at all. Round 1 did not surface this at all; it is a real gap in the original research, not an overblown concern invented for this stress test.

**Domain/social-handle saturation**: not independently re-verified this pass (no fresh registrar/handle check performed), carried forward from round 1's "unclaimed, low bar" finding with the caveat that an active company using the same name (Ilium Software) makes a genuinely clean `.com`/handle situation less likely than round 1 assumed — flagged as needing real verification before commitment, not assumed clean.

### 2. Dictionary/anatomy dominance and SEO ambiguity

Given the confirmed findings above, a bare search for "Ilium" almost certainly surfaces a genuine three-way split, not a clean anatomical result: the pelvic-bone/anatomy meaning, Ilium Software's own SEO-optimized company site, and Troy/Vonnegut literary content (Wikipedia's own "Ilium" and "Ilium (Kurt Vonnegut)" pages both rank highly for the bare term). This is a real, confirmed dilution of the "clean, ownable search term" premise round 1's recommendation rested on — **the word "Ilium" alone does not cleanly point at this product**, and never will without the app's own context (name paired with "AS & axSpA Tracker," a distinctive icon, App Store category) doing real disambiguating work. This is a materially weaker starting position than round 1 characterized it as.

### 3. Pronunciation, spelling, and word-of-mouth stress test

**English**: "ILL-ee-um" is the standard pronunciation, but the ileum confusion (§1) means a listener could plausibly mishear or misremember it as "ileum" specifically — a real, not hypothetical, risk given the two words' near-identical sound and the one-letter spelling difference.

**Turkish**: an average Turkish speaker with no Latin/English background has no strong existing intuition for "Ilium" — it requires the same explanation on first encounter every time, the same weakness round 1 already honestly flagged, now compounded by the fact that the correct spelling has to compete with a phonetically-similar but wrong "İlyum"/"İlyum" guess.

**Direct scenario test — "I use Ilium," said aloud:**
- **(a) Remember it?** Plausible but not strong — it's a real, "borrowed" word with existing meanings pulling at it (Troy, the software company, the anatomical term), which research on brand recall generally suggests makes a word *less* distinctively "sticky" as a NEW brand than either a fully invented word or a word with zero prior associations, precisely because the listener's memory has other, older claims on the same sounds.
- **(b) Spell it correctly in the App Store search bar?** Moderate risk — the ilium/ileum one-letter difference is a real, documented mix-up point, not a remote edge case.
- **(c) Find the right app on the first search?** Compromised — per §2, a bare "Ilium" search returns a real, unrelated software company and literary content alongside anatomy content; the user needs the fuller context (the App Store title's "AS & axSpA Tracker" portion) to land correctly, which is recoverable but is a real, confirmed cost round 1 did not price in.
- **(d) Understand from the word alone that it's a health/AS app?** No — honestly, no more than round 1 already conceded; "Ilium" alone communicates nothing about health without the subtitle doing the work.

### 4. Brand-distinctiveness/category-confusion test

Direct research did not find other pharma/biotech/medical-device companies specifically named "Ilium" (the Ilium Software collision is a non-medical category) — the pharma-naming-pattern risk flagged for "Vertra" in round 1 does not apply to "Ilium" in the same way (it doesn't carry an invented pharma-style suffix like "-tra"/"-ex"/"-in"). Ilium's confusion risk is real but is a *different* failure mode than "sounds like a drug" — it's "sounds like three unrelated real things," not "sounds clinical."

### 6. App Store naming architecture stress test

Not independently re-verified this pass with fresh searches (the earlier failed subagent had not yet reached this section when it hit the rate limit; re-running it was judged lower-priority than completing the higher-stakes collision work above under a constrained budget). Carrying forward round 1's own finding as the working assumption, flagged as needing a dedicated follow-up check before final commitment: real competitors (AS Log, Hurtl, Rapha) all lead with the abbreviation "AS" and/or "axSpA" in their titles/subtitles rather than spelling out "ankylosing spondylitis" in full, suggesting the abbreviated form is the category's own accepted convention, not a risk unique to this product's proposed title. §12's final naming-architecture recommendation proposes three variants precisely so a fully-spelled-out option is available as a hedge against this being wrong.

### Reasoning through a real reversal, shown honestly

At this point in the stress test, the evidence pointed toward replacing Ilium: three confirmed, real weaknesses (a live software-company collision, a documented medical-terminology confusion, a larger-than-expected literary association) against a name whose entire justification in round 1 was "real meaning, no real downside." That justification is now known to be false — Ilium has real downsides. The natural next move was to promote the cleanest-checking alternative instead. But re-checking the two strongest round-1 alternatives (Vertra, Marrow) under the same adversarial rigor found **both of them collide too — one worse than Ilium (Marrow, against a direct Pfizer health-app competitor), one comparably (Vertra, against an established consumer suncare brand)** — and Solace, added fresh as a promising warm-word candidate, turned out to be the single most collision-saturated name checked in this entire process.

**This is the actual finding this stress test needed to produce: "zero collision anywhere" is not an achievable bar for any real, meaningful word in 2026's saturated brand landscape — every serious candidate checked this round, Ilium included, has at least one real, live, unrelated-category collision.** The right decision criterion is therefore not "which name has no collision" (none do) but "which name's specific collision is most tolerable, weighed against what the name uniquely offers." On that basis: Ilium's collisions are diluted-search-visibility (Ilium Software, a productivity/utility app, not a health competitor) and a soft literary association (Troy) — both real costs, both non-health-category and therefore lower-stakes than Marrow's direct Pfizer health-app collision or Solace's multiple direct health-app collisions. The ileum/ileum confusion is the one finding that is specifically medical-context-relevant, and it is real — but it is also, honestly, a small, explainable, one-sentence-fixable confusion ("the bone, not the intestine"), not a structural collision with a competitor, and it can even be turned into a small, deliberate moment of brand storytelling (a memorable "wait, not that one" beat) rather than purely a liability — a genuine, if modest, reframe, not spin.

**Final naming decision: Ilium is confirmed, not replaced — but as a qualified, risk-aware decision, not the unexamined confidence of round 1.** See §12 for the explicit, binding decision statement and the required mitigations that come with it.

## 4. Logo stress test

**Challenging "The Held Line" (the Phase DESIGN-A recommendation) honestly, not defensively:**

- **At 24-32px**: the concept's actual differentiator — a stroke whose *texture* shifts from uneven/wavy to straight along its length — is a fine-grained detail that does not survive reduction. At true small size, waviness-vs-straightness is invisible; what remains is just "a curved line." That is a real, disqualifying flaw for an app-icon-scale mark, not a minor one.
- **Risk of reading as a generic swoosh**: yes, materially. A single curved stroke in a rounded field is one of the most common shapes in tech/fintech branding (growth arrows, "flow" motifs, checkmark-adjacent ticks). Nothing about the concept's *silhouette* (as opposed to its backstory) actually differentiates it from that crowded territory once the texture detail is lost.
- **Risk of reading as a smile**: real, if the curve's ends lift upward at all — an unintended second, cliché meaning (the exact "generic wellness-app happy-curve" the research explicitly wanted to avoid).
- **Risk of reading as a wave**: real, for the same reason — an unbroken curved stroke in a health/wellness context defaults, in a viewer's mind, to "calm water" absent a strong counter-signal.
- **Reliance on explanation**: the concept requires being *told* two separate things — "this is the shape of the ilium bone" and "the texture change represents irregularity resolving into steadiness" — stacked onto one simple stroke. A mark that needs a paragraph of backstory to justify its own shape is not doing its job. This is the concept's real, disqualifying weakness, and it does not survive honest scrutiny.

**Verdict: "The Held Line" does not survive the stress test as specified. It is replaced below**, not merely refined — the small-size failure and over-reliance on explanation are structural, not cosmetic.

### Three refined directions (at least one non-anatomical, as required)

**Direction 1 — "The Joint Mark" (anatomical, refined)**
- **Geometry**: two small, rounded capsule/pill shapes, slightly offset from one another, almost-but-not-quite touching at a deliberate angle — evoking the sacroiliac joint (where the ilium meets the sacrum: two bones in relation, not one bone's silhouette) rather than trying to draw a single bone's outline.
- **Silhouette**: two distinct rounded forms near each other — reads as "connection" or "two things in relation" even with zero anatomical context, which is *also* a real, useful meaning for a health-record product (patient and data; patient and doctor; past and present).
- **Positive/negative space**: the gap between the two forms is itself the meaningful part of the mark and can carry a subtle color shift (e.g., the accent color living specifically in that gap).
- **Small-size behavior**: robust — "two distinct rounded shapes near each other" remains legible even at 16px, because it doesn't depend on fine internal texture the way the original stroke concept did.
- **Icon-only behavior**: reads as a considered, geometric mark on its own, without requiring the wordmark alongside it to explain the shape.
- **Wordmark relationship**: the two-mark motif can echo in letterspacing or a distinctive double-dot treatment within the logotype.
- **Why ownable**: genuinely uncommon geometry in this category — not a stroke, not a ring, not a cross, not a smile-adjacent curve.

**Direction 2 — "The Margin Mark" (explicitly non-anatomical)**
- **Geometry**: a small, fixed device drawn from print/editorial convention — an asymmetric bracket-and-tick shape evoking a margin annotation or a journal's own corner dateline-stamp — built from two simple straight/gently-curved strokes at a fixed angle. No anatomical reference anywhere in the concept.
- **Silhouette**: reads immediately as "a mark" — punctuation- and margin-note-derived shapes are, by design, already legible at small/text sizes (this is exactly how quotation-mark-derived logos in journalism/editorial brands already work).
- **Positive/negative space**: the notch/gap in the bracket shape carries the accent color.
- **Small-size behavior**: excellent — the shape family it's drawn from is *designed* for small-scale legibility, unlike a subtle stroke-texture change.
- **Icon-only behavior**: its logic ("this is a margin/journal device") is graspable on sight, without narration — directly fixing "The Held Line"'s core, disqualifying flaw.
- **Wordmark relationship**: sits naturally as a raised/superscript mark beside or above the wordmark, the way an editorial mark sits beside a headline.
- **Why ownable**: nothing in the competitive set reviewed in Phase DESIGN-A (Oura's ring, Calm's mandala, Headspace's smiley, WHOOP's puck) uses an editorial/typographic-mark device — genuinely distinct territory, and it satisfies the requirement that at least one direction carry zero anatomical reference.

**Direction 3 — "The Settling Stroke" (anatomical idea kept, small-size flaw specifically fixed)**
- **Geometry**: a single curved capsule-shaped stroke, still tracing the ilium's own gentle wing-curve, but differentiated by *weight taper* (thick/heavy at one end, thin/light at the other) rather than by texture — weight gradients remain visible at small scale in a way fine waviness does not.
- **Silhouette**: a confident curved wedge — but honestly flagged as still carrying real residual risk of reading as a generic comma/teardrop shape at extreme reduction, a risk the other two directions don't share.
- **Small-size behavior**: meaningfully more robust than the original ("The Held Line"), since weight-taper survives reduction far better than texture-change — but still the most explanation-dependent of the three to fully land its meaning.
- **Why it's listed but not recommended**: it repairs the original concept's most acute technical flaw (small-size legibility) but not its deeper one (needing narration to feel intentional rather than decorative).

### Recommended final logo direction: **Direction 2, "The Margin Mark"**

It is the only one of the three that is simultaneously non-anatomical (satisfying the brief's own requirement), robust at true small size (the disqualifying flaw in the original concept), and legible as an intentional, considered mark without narration. It is also, on reflection, the *more coherent* choice architecturally: a mark justified by the chosen visual **territory** (Editorial Health Journal — a personal record, annotated like a margin note) is more durable than a mark justified by a single word's Latin etymology, especially given the naming decision itself is under fresh stress-test in this same document (§1-3) — a territory-derived mark survives a name change; an anatomy-derived pun tied to "Ilium" specifically would not.

## 5. Visual direction stress test — Editorial Health Journal vs. Modern Clinical Companion vs. a Hybrid

Three directions, specified concretely across the same five screens — not abstractly.

- **A — Editorial Health Journal** (Phase DESIGN-A's original recommendation): warm paper background, serif/slab headline accents, minimal cards, sparkline-scale data, journal-like Timeline.
- **B — Modern Clinical Companion**: light neutral background, single desaturated primary (ink-blue/teal/plum), tabular numerals everywhere, real restrained charts, precise structured layout.
- **C — Hybrid, "Warm Precision"** *(newly specified for this stress test)*: takes B's structural confidence and typographic discipline as its backbone — so it never feels soft or precious — but replaces B's cool primary with a warm neutral base plus one warm accent (never hospital-blue, never Territory A's editorial/serif affectation), uses real restrained charts and tabular numerals more readily than A, and treats Timeline as a clean structured record rather than a literal "journal" metaphor. In one sentence: B's bones with A's warmth — a considered modern product that happens to be warm, rather than a diary or an instrument.

### Reaffirm or change?

**Change, partially — adopt Hybrid C, "Warm Precision," as the new primary direction, replacing pure Territory A.**

Laying all three out screen-by-screen surfaced a real risk in Territory A that wasn't visible in the abstract: **the serif-headline, page-like, narrative-first treatment reads beautifully on a marketing page and risks reading as slow, precious, or under-functional on the screens people actually touch every single day** (Today, Check-in) — exactly the "fragile, not premium" failure mode this stress test's own §10 warns against. Territory B, on the other hand, is efficient and legible everywhere but never shakes a clinical coldness, confirmed across all five screens in the comparison above, and specifically wrong for Today/Check-in, which need warmth most.

Hybrid C resolves this directly: it keeps Territory A's warm palette and restraint (never hospital-blue, never a checklist-heavy paywall, never a chart-dashboard Today) while adopting Territory B's structural discipline for the screens that actually carry daily interaction load (Today, Check-in) and its document-grade precision for the one screen that genuinely needs it (Appointment Summary). The editorial instinct is not abandoned — it survives specifically on Timeline, where it is strongest and least risky (a browsing surface has room for a slower, page-like rhythm that a daily-action surface does not).

**Verdict: the "named exception" from Phase DESIGN-A (Territory B's precision for Appointment Summary/Labs only) is expanded, not discarded — Today and Check-in also adopt more of B's structural confidence than originally proposed, while keeping A's warmth and Timeline's editorial character intact.** This is a refinement of the original direction under real pressure-testing, not a reversal of its core instincts (warm, restrained, typography-led, non-clinical) — those survive completely; only the *degree* of "editorial slowness" applied to daily-use screens changes.

## 6-7. Screen-level design concepts + representative UI copy

*(The five concepts below are the FINAL, decided Hybrid-C ("Warm Precision") specifications — not another round of the three-way comparison above, which already did its job in §5. No production code. Copy is representative, final-feeling UI strings, not descriptions — both EN and TR, measured against the current product's copy density.)*

### Screen 1 — Today

**Hierarchy, top to bottom:**
1. A short greeting line, small, quiet, warm-gray.
2. Today's date, set plainly (no oversized display headline — the current app's `display`-size "How are you today?" headline is deliberately dropped; a question doesn't need to shout to be asked).
3. **One dominant object**: the day's check-in state.
   - Not yet done: a single warm, unmistakably primary button-sized module — no card border, just a warm-tinted full-width surface — with one short line of copy and the action itself. This is the only "highlighted" surface on the screen, matching the existing one-highlight-per-screen discipline, but simplified to remove the current implementation's eyebrow label + prompt sentence + separate yesterday-context line + button stack.
   - Already done: the logged values render as 2-3 large tabular numerals in a single row, no bordered card, just generous spacing — with the High-Symptom Day marker (if set) as one quiet warm-accent line beneath, and a single small text link to view/edit.
4. **Above the fold** (390×844, no scroll needed): greeting, date, and the check-in module/logged state. Nothing else needs to be visible without scrolling — this is a deliberate, named change from the current implementation, where the check-in card competes with due-medication rows for first-screen attention.

## 9. Design system reality check

Challenging each named element directly:

- **Terracotta**: real risk — muted terracotta/clay is an increasingly common "warm, not-generic-green" health-app accent choice as the whole category moves away from wellness-green; it is not, by itself, guaranteed distinctive in 2-3 years. **Mitigation**: terracotta's specific job (reserved for exactly one action per screen, never a decorative fill) matters more than the hue itself — but the hue should be tuned distinctively (a specific, slightly unusual terracotta-toward-rust value, tested against current competitor palettes at implementation time) rather than the most obvious "safe" clay tone.
- **Dusty gold**: low risk *because* it's used so rarely (milestone moments only) — its rarity is its own protection against genericness. No change.
- **Warm off-white**: real risk — "warm off-white background" is close to becoming the *new* default in premium consumer apps generally (not just health), the same way stark white was five years ago. **Mitigation**: the background alone cannot be the visual signature; it must be paired with something the rest of this section defines as genuinely distinctive (see the three signatures below) rather than relied on as distinctive by itself.
- **Rounded components**: real risk — every component in the current app is already rounded (12/16/20pt radii); rounding alone signals nothing. **Mitigation**: reduce reliance on rounded-container framing as the hierarchy device at all (per §7 of `docs/DESIGN_DIRECTION_2_0.md`, already the plan) — the fewer bordered/rounded containers appear on screen, the less this becomes a tell.
- **Pill controls**: real risk — pill-shaped chips/segmented controls are extremely common. **Mitigation**: the Check-in redesign in §6 above deliberately replaces pill/chip selection with large equal-width numeral/segment rows for the core scales specifically *because* pills are generic — pills should be retained only for genuinely secondary, infrequent controls (range selectors), not for the app's signature daily interaction.
- **List rows**: not a risk in itself — `ListRow`-style layout is simply how iOS content lists work; the risk was never the row, it was the *border-plus-fill-plus-radius* framing wrapped around groups of rows (already addressed in `docs/DESIGN_DIRECTION_2_0.md` §7).
- **Tabular numerals**: genuinely low risk and a real asset — this is a typographic *behavior*, not a visible "look," so it can't be screenshotted-and-copied the way a color or icon can; it's a durable, structural signature rather than a decorative one.

### The three visual characteristics that make a screenshot recognizable as this product, even with the logo removed

1. **Every recorded number is large and set in tabular numerals, with almost nothing else large on the screen.** No competitor reviewed in Phase DESIGN-A combines *radical numeral emphasis* with *near-total absence of decorative chrome around it* — WHOOP does big numerals but inside a denser, more instrumented layout; Apple Health does plain numerals but without any typographic distinctiveness at all. The combination — one or two oversized tabular numerals, otherwise a nearly bare screen — is the single most ownable, least-copied trait available to this system.
2. **The Health Timeline's continuous rail with variable-weight dots, never a card per event.** No reference product studied uses a single unbroken rail with dot-shape (not color-only) differentiating event significance — Day One groups by card-like date sections, Apple Health uses plain rows, no one in the set uses a literal continuous line with shape-varying markers. This is a genuinely uncommon structural device, screenshot-recognizable on sight.
3. **The near-total absence of bordered containers, replaced by whitespace and a single hairline rule per section.** This is a negative-space signature — precisely because "container-free" is *rare* (every competitor and the current app itself use bordered cards constantly), a genuinely container-light screen reads as distinctive by contrast alone, without needing a positive decorative device to do the work.

If a screenshot loses the logo and someone can still say "that's the AS app with the big numbers, the timeline rail, and no card borders" — the system has a real signature. If they can only say "it's warm and minimal," the system does not yet have one — this stress test's answer is the three specific, mechanical traits above, not a mood description, satisfying the brief's own bar for what counts as a real answer.

## 10. Accessibility stress test

Conceptually testing the Hybrid-C direction against a user experiencing strong fatigue, hand stiffness, high pain, reduced concentration, and large Dynamic Type simultaneously — the realistic worst-case combination, not one impairment in isolation:

- **Contrast**: the warm-on-warm risk flagged in Phase DESIGN-A (`docs/DESIGN_RESEARCH_2_0.md` §8) is real and gets worse under this exact user profile — reduced concentration and visual fatigue lower a person's tolerance for low-contrast text specifically. **Resolution**: primary text-on-background must be verified at real AA/AAA levels, not just "looks fine" — the ink tone should sit meaningfully darker than a typical "warm gray" so it survives both contrast testing and a tired eye; the terracotta accent must never be the only signal for anything (already a hard rule) and its own contrast against the warm background must be separately verified, since accent colors are the most commonly under-tested element in a palette.
- **Tiny metadata**: a real risk in an editorial-influenced system, where secondary/tertiary text (dates, captions, "12 entries" style counts) tends to shrink for hierarchy's sake. **Resolution**: no text anywhere in the system drops below the existing `caption` tier (13pt) regardless of visual role — hierarchy is carried by weight and color contrast, never by pushing text below a legible floor, and every size must scale correctly under large Dynamic Type settings without truncating (verified per-screen, not assumed).
- **Overly delicate typography**: the serif/slab accent originally proposed for Territory A's headlines is a real risk under this profile — thin/light serif weights read as elegant at rest and become genuinely harder to parse for someone fatigued. **Resolution**: confirmed by the Hybrid-C revision in §5 — the serif accent survives only on Timeline's date headers (a browsing context, lower cognitive load), and even there must use a medium-or-heavier weight, never a thin/light cut; it is fully removed from Today and Check-in, which is exactly where this risk would have mattered most.
- **Small editorial controls**: real risk — a "quiet, restrained" aesthetic can quietly shrink tap targets in pursuit of visual lightness. **Resolution**: every interactive control, without exception, holds the existing 44×44pt minimum regardless of how visually "light" its styling is — this is treated as non-negotiable across every screen in §6, including the Check-in redesign's large numeral/segment rows, which are explicitly *larger* than 44pt as a design feature, not just a compliance floor.
- **Gesture-heavy interactions**: directly addressed by the Check-in redesign's choice of discrete tap targets over any drag/slider control, and the body map's tap-only (never drag-to-paint) interaction — both already specified in §6, both hold under this stress test without modification.

**Overall verdict**: premium and accessible are not in tension in this system *as specified in §6* — but two specific elements of the original Phase DESIGN-A proposal (thin-weight serif accents, and an unverified warm-on-warm contrast palette) were real risks that this stress test catches and resolves, not risks that were already handled. Both fixes are now explicit, binding constraints on the design system, not aspirational notes.

## 11. Dark mode — Paper & Ink, not simply inverted

Direct definitions, not an inversion:

- **Background**: warm near-black (a true dark charcoal with a warm, not cool, undertone) — never pure black, which would remove the "paper" quality that makes the palette warm in light mode; the warmth must survive into dark mode or the palette's whole identity is lost the moment a user switches modes.
- **Surface**: a step lighter than background, still warm-charcoal family — barely distinguishable from background, exactly mirroring how light mode's surface is barely distinguishable from its own background (the relationship, not the literal values, is what carries over).
- **Elevated surface**: a further step lighter, with a soft warm-toned (never cool-gray) glow rather than a hard shadow — shadows read poorly on dark backgrounds generally, so elevation here is carried by a subtle lightness/glow step instead.
- **Ink/text hierarchy**: primary text becomes a warm off-white (not pure white — pure white on dark backgrounds causes halation/glare, a real legibility problem for exactly the fatigued/low-concentration user profile in §10); secondary and tertiary text step down in the same warm-neutral family, never shifting cool.
- **Terracotta behavior**: brightened and slightly desaturated from its light-mode value — a saturated terracotta that works on warm off-white can read as harsh/glowing on a dark background; the *role* (one reserved action color) stays identical, only the specific value shifts for dark-background legibility.
- **Gold behavior**: similarly brightened for the rare milestone-moment use case — since gold already appears so infrequently, this is a low-risk, low-effort adjustment.
- **Dividers**: lighten relative to the dark background (the inverse relationship from light mode, where dividers darken relative to a light background) — kept hairline-thin in both modes, never heavier in dark mode to "compensate," which would reintroduce the too-many-borders problem this whole reset is trying to remove.
- **Data visualization**: the labs reference-range band and chart lines both need independent dark-mode tuning, not a flat brightness inversion — a soft band that reads as "gentle context" in light mode can read as a harsh highlight if naively inverted; the same is true of the Timeline rail itself, which needs its own dark-mode contrast check since a thin light-gray rail on a warm dark background is a real legibility risk if not explicitly tuned (not just algorithmically inverted from its light-mode value).

**Verdict**: the palette does not fail in dark mode, but it cannot survive a naive inversion — every one of the seven elements above requires an explicit, separately-considered dark value, which this section now provides as a binding specification, not a placeholder.

## 12. Final decisions — explicit, no fence-sitting

**BRAND NAME: APPROVE ILIUM.**
Confirmed, not replaced, after real adversarial pressure that found three genuine weaknesses (an active, unrelated-category software-company collision; a documented ilium/ileum medical-terminology confusion; a larger-than-expected Troy/Vonnegut literary association) and, in the course of trying to replace it, found that **every serious real-word alternative checked collides too — one (Marrow) against a direct Pfizer health-app competitor, one (Solace) against multiple live health/wellness apps, one (Vertra) against an established consumer suncare brand** — none of them offering a cleaner profile than Ilium, and none of them matching Ilium's unique combination of real AS-specific meaning plus equal viability in both Turkish and English markets. **Required, binding mitigations, not optional polish**: (1) onboarding and the About screen must include one explicit, early sentence explaining the ilium-bone meaning, turning the ileum mix-up into a deliberate, memorable brand-story beat rather than leaving it to chance; (2) App Store copy must never rely on the bare word "Ilium" alone to carry category meaning — the "AS & axSpA Tracker" qualifier is now load-bearing, not decorative, given §2's confirmed search-dilution finding; (3) before any commercial commitment, run the real trademark/domain/social-handle clearance this document has repeatedly flagged as never having been performed — that check is now higher-stakes than round 1 assumed, given the confirmed Ilium Software collision.

**VISUAL DIRECTION: REPLACE PURE EDITORIAL HEALTH JOURNAL WITH HYBRID "WARM PRECISION."**
Per §5's five-screen comparison: pure Territory A reads beautifully as a concept but risks feeling slow/precious on the two screens that carry the most daily interaction load (Today, Check-in) — exactly the "premium must not mean fragile" failure this stress test was designed to catch. The named exception from Phase DESIGN-A (Territory B precision for Appointment Summary/Labs) is *expanded*, not discarded: Today and Check-in now also adopt more of Territory B's structural confidence, while the warm palette, restraint, and Timeline's editorial character survive completely intact. This is a refinement under pressure, not an abandonment of the original instinct.

**NAVIGATION: Today / Health Record / Appointments — three tabs, confirmed.**
Per §8's nine-criterion comparison against both the current four-tab model and a "Timeline"-labeled alternative. The alternative loses on one decisive, stress-test-surfaced point: naming the tab after its browsing function alone hides that it also manages Medications/Injections/Labs — a real first-week comprehension cost. "Health Record" correctly promises "several things live here." Knowledge moves out of this tab entirely (a genuine scope refinement this stress test surfaced, not present in the original plan) — it isn't health-record data, it belongs with Profile/supporting content instead.

**LOGO: "The Margin Mark" — the non-anatomical direction, confirmed as final.**
Per §4: the original recommendation ("The Held Line") does not survive scrutiny — its differentiating detail (stroke texture) is invisible at real icon size, and it requires narration to feel intentional rather than decorative, both disqualifying flaws for a mark that has to work alone on a home screen. "The Margin Mark" (an editorial/print-derived bracket-and-tick device) is chosen over the two anatomical refinements ("The Joint Mark," "The Settling Stroke") specifically because it is legible without explanation, robust at true small size, and — not incidentally — durable through exactly the kind of naming stress-test this document just ran: a mark justified by the chosen *territory* survives a name reconsideration; a mark justified by one word's Latin etymology would not have.

**FIVE SIGNATURE SCREENS: the §6 Hybrid-C ("Warm Precision") specifications move into implementation as written** — Today's single dominant check-in module above the fold with no metric grid; Check-in's one continuous large-target interaction with consistent row language across Pain/Stiffness/Fatigue; Timeline's continuous rail with shape-varying (never color-only) dots; Appointment Summary's document-grade, chart-only-for-labs structure; Paywall's one-sentence onboarding echo plus one real product preview replacing the feature checklist. These are the versions that move into Design-B/C/D/E/F of `docs/DESIGN_REDESIGN_PLAN_2_0.md`, not the abstract three-way comparison versions in §5.
